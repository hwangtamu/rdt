# E1 / E2 / E3-A — a consistent story

This note links the frozen-probe, fine-tuning, and rate–distortion results into
one narrative. The short version:

> The 68M encoder has a better absolute BoolQ decision representation than the
> decoder, but it does **not** have a demonstrably better rate–distortion
> frontier. The encoder advantage is an accessibility/absolute-quality
> advantage, not a compression-efficiency advantage. Readout, scale, and task
> remain first-order variables.

## 1. E1 — frozen probes: both backbones carry signal, but the readout matters

**Figures:** `fig01_e1_layer_68m.svg`, `fig02_e1_layer_150m.svg`

The layer-wise frozen-probe curves show three things:

- Both backbones carry usable BoolQ decision information.
- The signal rises with depth; early layers are near the label prior.
- Encoder and decoder are close under the last-token readout. At the last layer,
  encoder and decoder linear/MLP probes are within a few hundredths of a bit.
- The decoder improves more sharply in mid/late layers under the last-token
  readout; the encoder is more stable across depth.

This is an **accessibility** result, not a mutual-information result. A frozen
probe gap does not prove a representational information gap.

**Figure:** `fig03_e15_readout_68m.svg`, `fig04_e15_readout_150m.svg`

E1.5 changes the picture in an important way: **readout choice is first-order**.

- For the 68M encoder, attention pooling is clearly best: best frozen encoder
  cell is `attn-L18`, CE ≈ 0.858 (accuracy ≈ 0.695).
- For the 68M decoder, attention helps too: best decoder cell is `attn-L12`,
  CE ≈ 0.881 (accuracy ≈ 0.679).
- For the 150M pair, the best frozen cells are also readout-dependent: attention
  is strongest for the encoder, while the decoder’s best cell is a last-token
  MLP.

The conclusion is not “encoder always wins.” It is:

> Under the last-token readout the two backbones look close. Under a better
> frozen readout, the encoder can look stronger. Any E1 claim must be
> readout-matched.

**Figure:** `fig05_e1_cross_task.svg`

Frozen-probe transfer is task-dependent. The cross-task panel shows improvement
over the label prior for BoolQ, LevBench, When2Call, and ProofWriter. The signal
is strong on BoolQ/ProofWriter, moderate on LevBench, and **negative on W2C**
under a validation-selected probe. That negative result is a distribution-shift
caveat: the pref→MCQ shift is hard, and validation selection can choose a probe
that overfits the source distribution.

Takeaway from E1/E1.5:

1. Both models carry decision information.
2. The measured ranking depends on readout.
3. Frozen transfer is task-specific.
4. E1 is a baseline, not an information-efficiency measurement.

## 2. E2 — fine-tuning: the 68M encoder wins at full data, but not everywhere

**Figures:** `fig06_e2_scaling_68m.svg`, `fig07_e2_scaling_150m.svg`

E2 uses the same data fractions (5/10/25/50/100%) and five seeds. At 68M, the
matched `last-L19` readout gives a clear crossover:

- At low/mid data, the decoder is competitive or slightly better.
- At 100% data, the encoder overtakes it: CE ≈ 0.741 vs 0.778.

At 150M, the full-data ranking flips under the matched `last-L22` readout: the
decoder wins (CE ≈ 0.728 vs 0.834). Capacity and scale therefore change the
adaptation story.

**Figure:** `fig08_e2_delta_n.svg`

The ΔD plot makes the readout dependence explicit. At 68M:

- `last-L19`: decoder is better at low N, encoder better at 100%.
- `attn-L12`: encoder is better at the low end, decoder at 25%, encoder again
  from 50% onward.
- `attn-L18`: encoder is better throughout and the gap grows with N.

At 150M, `last-L22` favors the decoder strongly at 50–100%, with wide seed
variance.

So the consistent statement is:

> At 68M with the final-token readout, the decoder is sample-efficient at low
> data, but the encoder is better at full data. At 150M, the decoder can win at
> full data. The crossover is readout- and capacity-dependent.

**Figure:** `fig09_e2_learning_curves.svg`

The learning curves add an optimization caveat: at 100% BoolQ, training CE keeps
falling while validation CE rises after roughly epoch 1. Early stopping is doing
real work; E2 is not a converged fine-tuning comparison, it is a
validation-selected adaptation comparison.

**Figure:** `fig10_e2_readout_100.svg`

At 100% data, the 68M decoder strongly prefers the last-token readout
(`last-L19`), while attention readouts are worse. The encoder is more forgiving:
`last-L19` and `attn-L18` are close. This reinforces the E1.5 lesson: readout
choice is not a nuisance parameter.

E2 takeaway:

1. Fine-tuning changes the ranking relative to frozen probing.
2. The 68M encoder wins at full data under the matched last-token readout.
3. The decoder is competitive/sample-efficient at low data.
4. At 150M, the decoder can win at full data.
5. Readout and scale must be held fixed before drawing architecture conclusions.

## 3. E3-A — compression: the encoder is better, but not more compression-efficient

**Figures:** `fig11_e3a_absolute_rd.svg`, `fig12_e3a_delta.svg`,
`fig13_e3a_penalty.svg`

E3-A freezes the E2 checkpoints and puts a Gaussian VIB on the last-token
decision vector. After correcting feature scaling, checkpoint selection,
zero/high-rate controls, and matched-rate analysis:

- The encoder has lower expected CE at every matched rate from 1–8 bits.
- The gap is roughly 0.07–0.10 bits.
- But the compression penalty relative to each seed’s deterministic control is
  **not significantly different** between encoder and decoder.

At R = 2 bits:

| Quantity | Encoder | Decoder | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Matched expected CE | 0.793 | 0.866 | +0.073 (encoder better) |
| Compression penalty | +0.104 | +0.106 | +0.002 (CI includes 0) |

At R = 4 bits, the same pattern holds: +0.068 absolute advantage, ≈ −0.003
penalty difference with a CI including zero.

**Figure:** `fig14_e3a_ablation_delta.svg`

The ablation panel shows the absolute advantage is robust in **sign** across
latent 32, latent 128, no scaling, and per-dimension scaling. The magnitude is
not robust:

- Latent 128 gives a much larger apparent encoder advantage, but the decoder
  expected CE at low rates is near or above the prior, indicating a
  posterior-variance/optimization problem in the 128-d decoder VIB.
- No scaling gives a smaller apparent advantage; this is why the old P1 null
  result was not a clean test.
- Per-dimension scaling lies between.

So the honest conclusion is:

> Encoder advantage in absolute matched-rate CE is robust. Encoder advantage in
> compression penalty is not robust.

**Figure:** `fig15_e3a_variance.svg`

The expected-minus-mean-only gap is large at low rates, especially for the
decoder. That means expected stochastic CE is sensitive to posterior variance.
The mean-only diagnostic also favors the encoder in absolute CE, but it shows a
different penalty pattern. This is a modeling caveat, not a calibration failure.

E3-A takeaway:

1. The encoder is better at matched rate in absolute CE.
2. The encoder is not demonstrably more compression-efficient.
3. The result is closer to **Outcome B**: similar compression behavior,
   different absolute quality.
4. The old E3-A P1 null was a protocol artifact, not a stable scientific result.

## 4. The combined story

**Figure:** `fig16_story_summary.svg`

Across stages on 68M BoolQ:

- **Frozen E1.5 best:** encoder 0.858, decoder 0.881.
- **Fine-tuned E2 100%:** encoder 0.741, decoder 0.778.
- **Compressed E3-A at R=2:** encoder 0.793, decoder 0.866.

The encoder is consistently lower. But the **gap does not grow** in a way that
implies a better compression frontier: it is already present before compression,
and the compression-penalty difference is near zero.

The consistent interpretation is:

> The 68M encoder learns a better absolute BoolQ decision representation than
> the decoder under the tested readout. That advantage survives fine-tuning and
> compression. It does **not** become a robust information-efficiency advantage:
> the encoder is not significantly less compression-sensitive.

This is a nuanced result, not a failure:

- It rejects a strong “encoder is information-efficient” claim.
- It supports a weaker “encoder representation is better at this task/scale”
  claim.
- It explains why E1/E2 rankings depend on readout, data fraction, and scale.

## 5. What would change this story

1. **Token-level E3-B** — a valid pre-pooling VIB could reveal token-level
   organization differences that the pooled E3-A vector cannot see.
2. **Attention-L18 E3-A** — current E3-A uses only the final last-token
   representation; attention readout could change the compression curve.
3. **150M E3-A** — the 150M full-data decoder wins in E2; its E3-A curve may not
   follow the 68M pattern.
4. **Other tasks** — BoolQ is binary and clean; LevBench/W2C may show different
   compression behavior.
5. **Nested validation fix** — E2 checkpoint selection and E3 VIB early stopping
   currently share the BoolQ validation split. A fully disjoint E2-val/E3-val
   design would remove the remaining selection optimism.

Until those are run, the defensible claim is the scoped one above, not a general
statement about encoder architectures or mutual information.
