E3: Rate–Distortion Analysis of Ettin-68M Decision Representations1. ObjectiveResearch question: Do encoder and decoder architectures differ in how efficiently they preserve decision-relevant information under an explicit representation information budget?Our previous experiments established:E1 — Representation Accessibility: Frozen Ettin-68M encoder and decoder have relatively similar BoolQ probing performance.E2 — Task Adaptation: With 5 seeds and matched last-token readout, the decoder performs slightly better under limited supervision, while the encoder overtakes it with full supervision.E3 — Information Efficiency: Determine whether the encoder’s full-data advantage reflects better compression of decision-relevant information or simply a better uncompressed decision representation.E3 must measure an explicit information-rate proxy, rather than equating parameter count, hidden dimension, or training-set size with information rate.2. Include a higher-dimensional control, such as 128, if the bottleneck cannot recover the uncompressed performance.Use a small MLP or linear transformation for the Gaussian mean and log-variance. Mandatory Sanity ChecksCheck A — Zero-rate behaviorAt approximately zero transmitted rate, the bottleneck should be independent of the input.Prediction should approach the constant class-prior predictor.For our current BoolQ split, the reference label-prior CE is approximately:[D_{\mathrm{prior}}\approx0.9568\text{ bits}]Use the training-set label prior to compute the actual constant-predictor test CE.Check B — High-rate reconstruction of predictive performanceAt sufficiently weak compression, the bottleneck should approach the predictive performance of a comparable uncompressed decision head.Use two controls:Original E2 classifier without a bottleneck.A deterministic, trainable bottleneck and head with no KL constraint.The second control helps distinguish an information-budget limitation from insufficient capacity in the replacement bottleneck/head.A stochastic beta-zero configuration can be included as an additional control, but should not be used as a finite-rate point without measuring its KL.If the high-rate model cannot approach the deterministic control, increase latent dimension or investigate optimization before interpreting the rate–distortion curve.Check C — Rate monotonicityAs rate constraints become less restrictive, the optimized achievable distortion should generally decline.Individual runs may violate monotonicity because of finite optimization and sampling error.Do not silently smooth away these failures. Report raw measured points and identify unstable or dominated configurations.Check D — Prior and KL reliabilityThe KL term is an upper bound on mutual information and may be loose if the aggregate posterior differs substantially from the assumed prior.Run a sensitivity check with a more flexible, appropriately trained prior if the encoder–decoder conclusion depends strongly on the rate estimate.6. Statistical EvaluationFor the final E3-A analysis:Include all five E2 backbone seeds for each architecture.Repeat stochastic bottleneck training with multiple controlled initializations, preferably at least three per backbone seed for critical rate budgets.Use matched training data and test examples.Report mean, standard deviation, and confidence intervals.Use paired comparisons for example-level distortion differences.Account for both training-seed variation and test-example uncertainty.Avoid selecting final configurations using test performance.At matched rate budgets, compute:[\Delta D(R)D_{\mathrm{dec}}(R)-D_{\mathrm{enc}}(R)]Positive values favor the encoder.Evaluate whether the sign and magnitude remain stable across seeds and nearby rate budgets.Do not claim equality merely because a significance test fails to reject zero.8. Optional E3-C: End-to-End Bottleneck AdaptationOnly after completing E3-A and E3-B, consider allowing the backbone to update under the rate constraint.Optimize:[\min_{\theta,\phi,\psi}D+\beta R_{\mathrm{upper}}]This tests whether the entire model can reorganize its representation to satisfy an information budget.It answers a different question from E3-A:E3-A: How compressible are representations already learned during E2?E3-C: How efficiently can the model adapt its representations when trained under an information constraint?Keep these results separate.Do not combine frozen-backbone and end-to-end points into one rate–distortion curve.10. Interpretation FrameworkThree primary outcomes are possible.Outcome A — Encoder has lower distortion at matched rateIf the encoder consistently achieves lower distortion over the shared rate interval, the result supports an advantage in bottleneck-constrained prediction for the evaluated task and training protocol.However, distinguish this from the encoder’s pre-existing E2 performance advantage.Outcome B — Compression penalties are similarIf both architectures have similar compression-induced distortion while absolute performance remains different, their E2 advantage may primarily reflect task adaptation rather than different sensitivity to information compression.This does not prove their true mutual information is identical.Outcome C — Decoder is more compression-efficientIf the decoder requires a smaller measured information-rate budget to achieve a shared target distortion, the result challenges the hypothesis that encoder inductive bias necessarily yields a more compact decision representation.This is a scientifically meaningful outcome even if the encoder has better uncompressed accuracy.12. Execution Order and Stop ConditionsPhase 1 — E3-A PilotStart with Encoder-68M and Decoder-68M, 100% BoolQ, Last-L19.Use one validation-selected E2 checkpoint per architecture, three VIB initialization seeds, and the initial beta grid.First verify zero-rate behavior, high-rate recovery, and stable training.Phase 2 — E3-A Full SweepRun all five E2 seeds, refine the rate grid, and produce statistically supported rate–distortion comparisons.Phase 3 — Readout RobustnessRepeat selected common-rate conditions using Attention-L18. Check whether the architecture ranking depends on readout strategy.Phase 4 — E3-BRun token-level bottleneck experiments if the goal is to make claims about token-level information organization rather than only final decision-vector compression.Phase 5 — 150M External Scale ValidationReproduce critical comparisons on the 150M models, with matched readout definitions and explicit normalization controls.Completion criteriaE3 should be considered successful when:The bottleneck passes the zero-rate and high-rate sanity checks.Both architectures have adequate coverage of a shared rate interval.The reported curves are reproducible across seeds.The rate metric is properly identified as a variational upper bound.Differences in absolute prediction quality and compression sensitivity are analyzed separately.The final conclusions do not depend on a single beta, latent dimension, or favorable checkpoint.Final Research QuestionDoes the supervised decision-making advantage observed in Ettin-68M encoders correspond to a lower information budget required for accurate prediction, or does it mainly reflect differences in representation adaptation and readout compatibility?The purpose of E3 is to distinguish these explanations—not to assume that the encoder must win.
